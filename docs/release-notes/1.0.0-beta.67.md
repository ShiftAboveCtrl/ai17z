AI17Z can price a real market. Paper trading stops being a form that always
answers "no market" and becomes a trade that fills against a real pool, at a
real price, with the real fee the pool charges.

## A market reader that needs nothing

`packages/runtime/src/poolMarketReader.ts` reads public automated market maker
pools through the market indexer this repository already carried. **No
credential, no RPC endpoint, no custody and nothing to install.** It asks a
public API the same thing a person asks by opening a web page.

The obstacle was never the data. `TRADE_VENUES` had no generic pool venue, so
there was nothing for a reader to register against: every venue named was one
whose adapter is deliberately not in this repository. `AMM_POOL_EVM` and
`AMM_POOL_SOLANA` are that venue, named for the mechanism rather than for an
operator, and kept distinct from Pons and Pump, whose arithmetic and
transaction shapes are their own.

## What reading a real market found

Four of these could not have come from a fixture, because a fixture contains
the pools somebody thought of.

**The deepest pool is the wrong pool.** The deepest pool holding WETH on
Ethereum was a three-asset Curve pool at 827,039,486 USD, against the
WETH/USDC cluster at about 96,000,000 — and what an indexer reports for it is
a two-asset projection of a three-asset invariant, which is not what a pair
trade fills at. Pools are therefore read as a population: the median price is
what the token is worth, and the pool quoted is the one nearest that median.
One pool cannot move a median, and the answer is still one real pool, because
a trade fills somewhere rather than everywhere.

**A pool that will not say what it charges is not priceable.** The fee is
absent for exactly the pools whose pair price is a projection. Absent is not
zero: charging nothing simulates a fill better than any real one, which is the
one direction a simulation must never err. The risk gate was missing the
matching rule, so `FEE_UNKNOWN` now sits beside `LIQUIDITY_UNKNOWN`.

**A true price in the wrong unit is the hardest kind of wrong to see.** Asked
about WETH without being told what to price it in, the representative pool was
a WETH/WBTC pool: a correct price, in Bitcoin, for a trade denominated in
dollars, which the floor calculation then turned into a limit out by four
orders of magnitude. A market read now carries what the price is wanted in,
and a reader that cannot answer in it declines rather than substituting.

**A quote cannot be from the future because reading took time.** The paper
engine took the current time once on entry, so the milliseconds its own two
reads took made its own fresh quote look like it came from the future. Every
test passed, because a fake reader answers instantly with a timestamp written
down earlier.

**And one much larger, found while testing the others.** Validating an amount
threw instead of refusing: every amount in AI17Z goes through one rule, and a
decimal point in any limit, transfer, mandate ceiling or trade size was
answered with a server error rather than a sentence explaining what an amount
is.

## Shadow trading

The same pipeline, against live markets, on a schedule, executing nothing. It
exists to answer the question nobody can answer from a description: given this
venue, this pair and this size, what would actually have happened, repeatedly,
over days.

It is **not a strategy** and will not become one. An owner writes down a pair,
a size and how often; nothing here decides what is worth trading. What it
measures is execution and market conditions. There is no capability for it and
there must not be, because a model that could create a standing instruction to
read a market every minute has been handed a schedule.

No second engine and no second scheduler: a due shadow calls the one paper
pipeline, and the claim moves the next run forward in the statement that
selects the row, like every other loop here. A global pause stops it before it
spends a read. Every outcome is recorded, including the ones that went wrong,
because a shadow nobody can see failing is worse than one that is not running.

## A port nothing is listening on that nothing may bind

Both installations here had run for weeks on their web ports. Neither would
start one morning, and nothing about either had changed: Windows had reserved
the block of ports around them for its own virtualisation. Nothing listens on
a reserved port and nothing may bind it, so the launcher's check said the port
was free and Docker then failed with a message about socket permissions.

The launcher now recognises this, names the reserved block, says which setting
to change, and gives the command that lists every block. The blocks move when
a machine reboots, so a port chosen at install time is not a port that stays
usable: this could not be only an installer check.
