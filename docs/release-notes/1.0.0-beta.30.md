**An audit of what was actually wired to what, and the things it found.** Beta 3.5 through 3.9 added capabilities. This one went looking for the places where two of them should have been talking and were not, and fixed the ones that mattered.

Most of what follows was invisible from the outside, which is why it lasted.

## Your agent recognises what a post is about

An agent's topics are how it says what it follows. There were two pieces of code deciding whether a post matched them, and they disagreed.

The one deciding whether to **like** something required the whole topic, word for word. An agent that follows "browser automation" therefore never once recognised a post about a renderer, because nobody writes "browser automation" in a sentence. On a real account, a week of decisions scored on who wrote the post and how recent it was, and essentially never on what it was about. The agent was liking people it knew rather than things it follows.

The one deciding whether to **reply** was more generous, and too generous in a different way: it counted any word of four letters or more, and a topic phrased the way a person speaks contains ordinary words. "what agents get wrong" contributed "what", so "what time does the match start tonight" was on-subject for an agent that follows browser automation. That is precisely the stranger's football post the rule exists to prevent, arriving through the check meant to stop it.

There is one matcher now, it ignores words that carry no subject, and both decisions use it.

**And somebody your agent has actually spoken to is no longer read as a stranger.** A regular correspondent, sixteen exchanges in, asking whether a bug had been fixed, was scored "nothing to do with what this agent follows" on the strength of their vocabulary. A person who keeps writing to you is a reason to pay attention, the same way attaching a project is.

## A record of what has been done, that you can read

Fifty-three places in AI17Z record who did what: approving a reply, connecting an account, pausing everything. Nothing showed you any of it, and the address that sounded like it would returned something else entirely.

That was untidy until Telegram grew commands, and then it was a gap: a paired chat can pause every agent and decide what they send, and a record nobody can read is not a record. **Settings now has "What has been done"**, newest first, filterable by which part of AI17Z it came from.

## One answer to whether anything is wrong

The Health screen weighed the database, the providers, the models, the channels, the workers, the browser and the machine's memory. Telegram's `/health` counted workers and unread problems and knew about none of the rest.

So your phone could tell you everything was fine while the screen said a provider had stopped answering. They are the same report now. The phone shows you the parts that are unwell, because a list of twelve healthy rows is the same as no message.

## A browser budget that is kept

AI17Z works out what your machine can afford. It was working out seven numbers and honouring two.

The one that mattered: on a machine under 10 GB it decided two live X tabs, and then opened four anyway, each allowed a large share of its own memory ceiling, beside Docker and Postgres. **That limit is now kept**, by closing the tab nobody is using and reopening it when it is needed again, which is something AI17Z already does for other reasons.

The four numbers nothing could ever act on are gone, including one whose description said it was passed to the worker at startup and never was. The Health screen used to print a browser ceiling AI17Z did not check; it now says what is actually applied.

## Smaller things

**A table that was written for months and read never** has been retired. Everything it held was already recorded, a line or two away, somewhere you can see.

**Rehearsals stay out of your inbox and your mentions**, which Beta 3.7 fixed, and the checks for it are now part of the release gate.

**Four packages were importing code they never declared.** It worked, which is why nobody noticed.

## For anyone reading the code

There is a new document, *How something gets from the world to X and back*, which maps the path from an observation to a published action and answers the questions people actually ask: where X reading happens, where actions are executed, where approvals are decided, where health is classified. It exists because this release kept finding the same shape: two implementations of one idea, each cheap to fix and expensive to find.
